Does Medicare Pay for Assisted Living? What It Covers

It is one of the first questions families ask when a parent starts needing more help: will Medicare pay for assisted living?

November 8, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 6 min read

It is one of the first questions families ask when a parent starts needing more help: will Medicare pay for assisted living? The short answer is no. Medicare does not pay for the room, meals or daily personal care that make up most of what assisted living provides. But Medicare still matters a great deal during a move to assisted living, because it continues to cover your parent's doctors, hospital care, prescriptions and several kinds of care that can happen right where they live.

Medicare open enrollment runs from October 15 through December 7, 2026, with changes taking effect January 1, 2027, so this is a good time to understand what the program does and does not do. This guide walks through it in plain language.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice. Coverage depends on your parent's specific plan and situation.

Why Medicare does not cover assisted living

Medicare is health insurance. It pays for medical care: hospital stays, doctor visits, tests, therapy and prescriptions. Assisted living is mostly what Medicare calls "custodial care," meaning help with everyday activities such as bathing, dressing, eating, using the bathroom and taking medications, along with housing and meals.

According to Medicare's long-term care coverage page, Medicare does not cover long-term care or custodial care when that is the only care a person needs, and that includes care in assisted living. The same page notes that Medicare Supplement Insurance (Medigap) policies do not cover it either. Medicare also does not pay for room and board in an assisted living community.

That surprises many families, especially adult children who assumed Medicare would step in once a parent turned 65. Knowing this early gives you time to plan instead of scrambling after a hospital stay.

What Medicare does cover when a parent lives in assisted living

Moving into assisted living does not end your parent's Medicare. Their coverage continues, with one thing to check: if the new address is outside a Medicare Advantage or Part D plan's service area, the plan may need to change. Here is what typically stays in place.

Doctors, hospital care and prescriptions

Part A still covers inpatient hospital care, and Part B still covers doctor visits, outpatient services, lab work, preventive care and durable medical equipment such as walkers or wheelchairs when they are medically necessary. If your parent has a Part D drug plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, their prescriptions are covered the same way they were at home.

Home health services

Medicare can cover part-time or intermittent skilled care, such as skilled nursing or physical, occupational or speech therapy, delivered where your parent lives. To qualify, Medicare's home health rules require that a doctor certify the need, that the care be part-time or intermittent, and that the person be considered "homebound." Medicare does not pay for 24-hour care at home or for the custodial help that is the core of assisted living.

Hospice care

If a parent has a terminal illness and a life expectancy of six months or less, Medicare's hospice benefit can usually be received where they live, including an assisted living community. Hospice covers the care team, medications for comfort and related supplies. It does not pay for room and board.

Short-term skilled nursing after a hospital stay

This is the coverage most often confused with long-term care. Medicare Part A can pay for a short stay in a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation after a qualifying hospital stay. Per Medicare's skilled nursing facility coverage page, the key rules are:

  • A medically necessary inpatient hospital stay of at least three days in a row. Time spent "under observation" or in the emergency room before admission does not count.
  • Admission to a Medicare-certified skilled nursing facility, generally within about 30 days of leaving the hospital.
  • A need for daily skilled care, such as therapy or skilled nursing.
  • Coverage of up to 100 days per benefit period, with a daily coinsurance charge after day 20 under Original Medicare.

This is rehab, not a permanent home. Once skilled care is no longer needed, coverage ends, and many families face the question of what comes next.

What about Medicare Advantage?

Medicare Advantage plans, offered by private insurers approved by Medicare, cover everything Original Medicare does and sometimes add extra benefits. Some plans have offered limited supplemental benefits such as in-home support, meals or transportation. These extras vary widely from plan to plan, and they do not pay for assisted living rent or care.

Benefits can also change from year to year. During open enrollment, look at your parent's Annual Notice of Change, which the plan sends each fall, and compare options on Medicare Plan Finder. If your parent is likely to need rehabilitation after a hospital stay, check how their plan handles skilled nursing stays and which facilities are in its network, since network rules can shape which options are available.

Questions to ask during open enrollment

If you help a parent choose a plan, these questions are worth asking before December 7:

  • Are my parent's current doctors and specialists in the plan's network?
  • Are their prescriptions covered, and at what tier?
  • How does the plan cover home health, therapy and skilled nursing stays after a hospitalization?
  • Does the plan require prior authorization for rehab or home health?
  • What extra benefits does the plan offer for 2027, and what are the limits?
  • What are the total yearly costs, including premiums, deductibles and copays?

You do not have to sort through this alone. Tennessee's State Health Insurance Assistance Program (TN SHIP) offers free, unbiased Medicare counseling to people with Medicare, their families and caregivers. SHIP counselors do not sell or endorse plans.

So how do families pay for assisted living?

Because Medicare does not cover it, most families pay for assisted living through a combination of private resources. Common pieces include monthly income such as Social Security and pensions, savings and investments, the proceeds from selling a home, and benefits from a long-term care insurance policy if a parent owns one.

Government programs such as Medicaid (TennCare CHOICES in Tennessee) and VA benefits have their own eligibility rules, and they work differently from one community and one situation to the next. Before counting on any program, confirm the details with a benefits counselor or an elder law attorney.

The Pointe at Kirby Gate is a private-pay community, and our rates are published on our website so families can plan with real numbers. For a deeper look at the options, our guide to paying for senior living walks through income, savings, home equity, insurance and tax considerations step by step.

Plan before a hospital stay forces the decision

Many families first learn that Medicare does not cover assisted living at the worst possible moment: when a parent is about to be discharged from the hospital or a rehab stay is ending. A little planning now can make that moment far less stressful.

  1. Review your parent's Medicare coverage during open enrollment, and write down their plan name, member number and doctors.
  2. Gather financial information: monthly income, savings, property and any insurance policies.
  3. Talk with your parent about what they would want if living at home became harder.
  4. Visit a few communities before you need one, so you know what feels right.
