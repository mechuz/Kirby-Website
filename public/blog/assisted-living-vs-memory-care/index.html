Assisted Living vs. Memory Care: What's the Difference?

When a parent starts needing more help, two terms come up again and again: assisted living and memory care.

November 4, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 7 min read

When a parent starts needing more help, two terms come up again and again: assisted living and memory care. They sound similar, and many communities offer both under one roof, so it is easy to wonder whether the difference is real or just a label. It is real. The two are built for different needs, and choosing the right one early can spare your parent, and you, a second move later.

This guide explains what each one is, how they differ day to day, the signs that point toward memory care, and the questions worth asking on a tour.

What assisted living is

Assisted living is for older adults who want to keep as much independence as possible but need help with some daily tasks. The National Institute on Aging describes it as a fit for people who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides.

A typical assisted living day includes:

  • A private apartment or suite, with meals served in a shared dining room
  • Help with personal care such as bathing, dressing and grooming, as needed
  • Medication reminders or help taking medicines
  • Housekeeping, laundry and maintenance handled for the resident
  • A calendar of activities, outings and social time

Residents come and go within the community freely, choose how much to join in, and keep their own routines. The care is there when they need it and stays in the background when they do not.

In Tennessee, assisted living communities are licensed as assisted-care living facilities by the state's Health Facilities Commission, under the rules in Chapter 0720-26 of the Tennessee rules. That licensing is different from a nursing home license, and assisted living is not a nursing home or a skilled nursing facility.

What memory care is

Memory care is a setting designed specifically for people living with Alzheimer's disease or another form of dementia. It is often a dedicated neighborhood, wing or floor within a larger community. The Alzheimer's Association notes that these special care units are often a cluster of residents in one area of a larger building, and they may be secured or unsecured depending on the community.

What sets memory care apart is not one feature but the way everything is designed around memory loss:

  • Safety and layout. Secured entries and exits help prevent wandering, and the space is laid out to be easy to find your way around, with fewer confusing hallways and less noise.
  • Staff trained in dementia care. Team members learn how to approach someone who is confused or upset, how to redirect gently, and how to communicate when words are harder to find.
  • Structured, predictable days. Routines reduce anxiety. Meals, activities and rest follow a steady rhythm.
  • Activities built for engagement. Music, simple hands-on tasks, reminiscence and small-group activities are chosen so people can take part at their own level.
  • More hands-on support. Residents often need more cueing and help with everyday tasks, so staff are more present throughout the day.

The key differences, side by side

Who it serves

Assisted living serves people who are generally oriented and safe on their own for stretches of the day but need help with some tasks. Memory care serves people whose memory loss affects their safety, judgment or ability to manage daily life, even with reminders.

Safety and freedom of movement

Assisted living residents move freely in and out of the community. Memory care usually uses secured doors and enclosed outdoor spaces so residents can walk and spend time outside safely, without the risk of getting lost.

Staffing and training

Both settings have staff available, but memory care teams receive training focused on dementia. The Alzheimer's Association recommends asking whether a community's staff are trained in dementia care and whether those credentials are verified.

Daily life

In assisted living, residents choose from a calendar and plan their own days. In memory care, staff guide the day more actively, inviting residents into activities rather than waiting for them to sign up.

Cost

Because memory care involves more staff time, dementia training and a specialized setting, ask each community how its memory care rate compares with its assisted living rate. The National Institute on Aging notes that most people pay the full cost of assisted living themselves. Ask every community for its current rates in writing, and compare what is included. Our own rates are published on our rates and floor plans page, and our guide to paying for senior living explains common ways families plan for the cost.

Signs your parent may need memory care rather than assisted living

A diagnosis of dementia does not automatically mean memory care. Many people in the early stages do well in assisted living with reminders and support. The question is whether your parent can stay safe and engaged in a less structured setting. Signs that point toward memory care include:

  • Wandering, or getting lost on familiar routes or in familiar buildings
  • Leaving the stove on, doors unlocked or water running
  • Missing medicines or taking them twice, even with reminders
  • Growing confusion in the late afternoon or evening
  • Withdrawing from activities because they have become hard to follow
  • Changes in mood or behavior that a less structured setting cannot support
  • A family caregiver who is exhausted and cannot keep up

Our article on 10 signs it may be time for memory care goes into each of these in more detail. Your parent's doctor is the right person to talk with about what you are seeing; a care assessment from the community can also help match the setting to your parent's needs.

What if your parent needs something in between?

Needs change over time, and that is one of the strongest reasons to look at communities that offer both assisted living and memory care in one place. If your parent moves into assisted living and later needs more support, a move to memory care within the same community means familiar faces, the same dining room and a much gentler change than starting over somewhere new.

Some families also use a short respite stay as a trial. It gives your parent a chance to experience the community, and gives the care team a chance to see which setting fits best. Our respite care page explains how short stays work.

Questions to ask on a tour

Whichever setting you are considering, a tour tells you more than any brochure. Bring these questions:

  • How do you decide whether someone belongs in assisted living or memory care?
  • What dementia training do staff receive, and how often is it refreshed?
  • How is the memory care area secured, and can residents get outside safely?
  • What does a typical day look like in memory care? Can I see an activity in progress?
  • How do you handle a resident who is upset or confused?
  • How are families involved in care planning, and how will you tell me about changes?
  • If my parent's needs change, can they move from assisted living to memory care here?
  • What is included in the monthly rate, and what costs extra?

The Alzheimer's Association also suggests noticing whether staff encourage residents to take part in activities rather than simply offering them, and whether the setting is calm and easy to move through. Our full list of questions to ask on an assisted living tour includes a printable checklist.
