10 Signs It May Be Time for Memory Care

Few families decide on memory care in a single moment. More often, small changes pile up over months: a missed medication, a pot left on the stove, a phone call at 3 a.m.

October 30, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 7 min read

Few families decide on memory care in a single moment. More often, small changes pile up over months: a missed medication, a pot left on the stove, a phone call at 3 a.m. Each one is easy to explain away on its own. Together they tell a story.

If you are reading this late at night after a hard day with a parent, you are not alone, and you are not failing anyone by asking the question. This guide walks through ten signs families most often describe, what each one can mean, and some gentle next steps.

There is no single right moment

Guidance from the National Institute on Aging describes what this point can look like: a person living with Alzheimer's may need care around the clock, may be incontinent or aggressive, or may wander a lot, and it may no longer be possible to meet all of their needs at home. In the end, the decision comes down to two things: what your parent needs, and whether the people caring for them can still meet those needs safely at home.

That framing matters. The question is not whether you love your parent enough to keep going. It is whether home is still the safest, calmest place for them, and for you.

Ten signs worth paying attention to

1. Wandering or getting lost

According to the Alzheimer's Association, six in ten people living with dementia will wander at least once, and many do so repeatedly. Wandering can happen at any stage of the disease. If your parent has left the house at night, gotten lost on a familiar drive, or tried to "go home" while already at home, take it seriously. One frightening afternoon is often what moves a family from wondering to acting.

2. The house is no longer safe

Burners left on, doors left unlocked, a space heater pushed too close to the curtains, stairs that have become a hazard. The Alzheimer's Association's home safety checklist suggests steps such as stove knob covers, appliances with an automatic shut-off, and keeping medications locked away. When the list of safety workarounds keeps growing, that is a sign in itself.

3. Medications are missed, doubled or mixed up

Pill organizers help for a while. But when you find a week of pills untouched, or realize your parent took a morning dose twice, medication mistakes have become a safety concern. Ask your parent's doctor or pharmacist to review what happened; they can tell you how serious a particular mistake is and what would make the routine safer.

4. Falls, or near-falls, are becoming common

Falls are common in later life for everyone: in a CDC analysis of 2020 survey data, 27.6 percent of adults 65 and older said they had fallen in the past year. A person living with memory loss may also forget to use a walker, misjudge a step, or try to get up alone at night. If falls are adding up, or your parent cannot reliably call for help after one, the picture has changed.

5. Bathing, dressing and grooming are slipping

The same clothes for days, skipped showers, unwashed hair: changes in personal care are often easy for families to spot. They can come from forgetting, from a fear of falling in the shower, or from no longer being sure of each step. Assisted living and memory care typically include help with daily tasks such as bathing, dressing and medications.

6. Weight loss or missed meals

Open the refrigerator. Expired food, untouched meals from a delivery service, or a parent who insists they ate when they clearly did not can all point to trouble with planning, cooking or simply remembering to eat. Unexplained weight loss is always worth a call to the doctor.

7. Agitation, outbursts or late-day confusion

The Alzheimer's Association describes the middle stage of dementia as a time when changes such as outbursts and disrupted sleep can appear. Some people grow more confused and restless from dusk into the night, a pattern many families know as sundowning. These changes come from the disease, not from anything you or your parent did wrong, but they are hard to manage alone, especially at night.

8. Your parent is withdrawing

Days without a real conversation. A circle of friends that has quietly fallen away. Hobbies abandoned because they have become frustrating. Isolation is easy to miss because it is quiet. A setting with daily structure, familiar faces and activities planned for people living with memory loss is built to offer the kind of connection that has slipped away at home.

9. Nights have become the hardest part

Up at 2 a.m., then up again at 4. Trying to leave the house in the dark. Calling you because they are not sure where they are. When a parent's nights are disrupted, so are yours, and exhaustion makes every other part of caregiving harder.

10. The caregiver is running on empty

This sign is about you. Nearly 13 million Americans provide unpaid care for someone living with Alzheimer's or another dementia, according to the Alzheimer's Association, and the work grows over time. One study cited in the Association's 2026 Facts and Figures report found that people living with dementia needed about 151 hours of care a month at the onset of dementia, rising to about 283 hours a month eight years later. If you have stopped sleeping, stopped seeing friends, or started dreading the phone, your well-being counts too. Choosing memory care is often how families get back to simply being family.

What memory care adds

Memory care is residential care designed specifically for people living with Alzheimer's disease and other dementias. Every community is different, but families can generally expect:

  • A layout designed to keep residents safe from wandering while still letting them move about freely.
  • Caregivers trained to understand and respond to the changes that come with memory loss.
  • A predictable daily rhythm of meals, rest and activities.
  • Help with bathing, dressing, medications and meals, with staff on hand day and night.
  • Room for families to visit as sons and daughters again, rather than as full-time caregivers.

When you tour, ask each community to show you how it does each of these things on an ordinary day.

Starting the conversation

Talking with a parent about memory care is rarely one conversation. A few things tend to help:

  • Talk with your parent's doctor first, and ask for an honest read on what your parent needs now and what is likely to change.
  • Bring siblings and other family into the decision early, so no one feels blindsided later.
  • Visit together on a calm day, and stay for a meal.
  • Keep the focus on safety, comfort and connection rather than on what your parent can no longer do.

A short stay can be a gentle first step

Some families begin with a respite stay: a short, fully furnished stay that lets a parent try the routines, the meals and the people, and gives the caregiver a real break. At The Pointe at Kirby Gate, respite stays start at a five-night minimum; ask us whether a short stay is a good fit for your parent. A trial stay can answer questions that a tour cannot.

A printable checklist

Print this and check off what you have noticed in the past month. Bring it to your parent's next doctor's appointment, and to any community you tour.

  • Wandered, got lost, or tried to leave the house at night
  • Left the stove on, the door unlocked, or other safety slips at home
  • Missed, doubled or mixed up medications
  • Fell, nearly fell, or could not get up without help
  • Stopped bathing, changing clothes or grooming as before
  • Lost weight or skipped meals
  • Agitation, outbursts or more confusion late in the day
  • Withdrawn from friends, hobbies or conversation
  • Disrupted nights for your parent, and for you
  • You, the caregiver, feel exhausted, isolated or worried about your own health

Help is available, day or night

The Alzheimer's Association offers a free 24/7 Helpline for families with questions about dementia. Your parent's doctor is also an important partner in deciding what comes next.
