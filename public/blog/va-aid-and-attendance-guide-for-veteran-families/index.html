VA Aid and Attendance: A Guide for Veteran Families

Veterans Day has a way of bringing family history to the surface. Maybe it's Dad's old service photo on the mantel, or a conversation with Mom about the years she spent as a Navy nurse.

November 11, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 8 min read

Veterans Day has a way of bringing family history to the surface. Maybe it's Dad's old service photo on the mantel, or a conversation with Mom about the years she spent as a Navy nurse. And if you're starting to look at assisted living for a parent who served, you've probably heard someone say, "Look into Aid and Attendance."

It's good advice, but the benefit is often misunderstood. This guide walks through what VA Aid and Attendance actually is, who may qualify, how much it pays right now, and how to apply without paying anyone to do it. It's written for the adult child doing the research, often late at night, with a lot on their mind.

This article is general information, not legal, financial or benefits advice. VA rules are detailed and personal, so please confirm your parent's situation with a VA-accredited representative or benefits counselor.

What Aid and Attendance is (and isn't)

Aid and Attendance is not a separate program. It is an increased monthly amount added to a VA pension for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities, according to VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound page (checked November 9, 2026). The payments are tax-free.

A few points clear up most of the confusion:

  • It's paid to the veteran or surviving spouse. The money goes to your parent, not to a doctor, home care agency or senior living community. Your parent decides how to use it in their monthly budget.
  • It's needs-based. Because it builds on the VA pension, your parent has to meet income and net worth limits, not just medical need.
  • It isn't the same as VA disability compensation. Disability compensation is for conditions connected to military service. Pension, including Aid and Attendance, is for wartime veterans with limited income, whatever caused their health needs.
  • It usually doesn't cover the whole bill. It can be a meaningful piece of a family's budget, but it's rarely the only piece.

Who may qualify

There are three parts to eligibility: military service, medical need, and finances. Your parent generally needs to meet all three.

1. Wartime service

According to VA's Veterans Pension eligibility page (checked November 9, 2026), a veteran generally needs a discharge that wasn't dishonorable and at least 90 days of active duty, with at least one day during a recognized wartime period. Veterans who started active duty after September 7, 1980, generally need 24 months of service or the full period they were called to serve, with some exceptions. Combat isn't required; serving during a wartime period is what matters. VA lists the exact wartime dates on the same page.

The veteran also needs to be 65 or older, or meet another condition VA lists, such as a permanent and total disability.

Surviving spouses of wartime veterans may qualify for Survivors Pension with Aid and Attendance, with their own rate table on VA's Survivors Pension rates page (checked November 9, 2026).

2. A need for help with daily life

VA's Aid and Attendance page says your parent may qualify if at least one of these is true:

  • They need another person's help with everyday activities like bathing, feeding and dressing.
  • They have to stay in bed, or spend a large part of the day in bed, because of illness.
  • They live in a nursing home because they've lost mental or physical abilities related to a disability.
  • Their eyesight is limited to a level VA defines on the same page, even with glasses or contacts.

A related benefit, Housebound, applies when someone is largely confined to home because of a permanent disability. VA says you can't receive Aid and Attendance and Housebound at the same time.

3. Income and net worth limits

For December 1, 2025, through November 30, 2026, the net worth limit for Veterans Pension is $163,699, according to VA's pension rates page (checked November 9, 2026). Net worth counts both assets and yearly income. VA does not count your parent's primary home, their vehicle or basic household items.

Two rules catch many families off guard:

  • Medical and care costs can lower countable income. Out-of-pocket medical expenses above a small threshold can be subtracted from income when VA figures the benefit. Under VA's 2018 pension rule (Federal Register, September 18, 2018), this can include certain costs of care, but VA has specific rules about which care and housing costs count. An accredited representative can tell you how they apply to your parent.
  • There's a three-year look-back. VA reviews assets given away or sold for less than they're worth during the 36 months before a claim. Transfers that would have put your parent over the net worth limit can lead to a penalty period of up to five years, under 38 CFR 3.276 (eCFR, checked November 9, 2026). Talk to an accredited representative before moving money.

How much Aid and Attendance pays right now

VA publishes a Maximum Annual Pension Rate, or MAPR. Your parent's payment is generally that maximum minus their countable income, which is why two veterans with the same needs can receive very different amounts.

For December 1, 2025, through November 30, 2026, VA's rate tables list these maximums with Aid and Attendance (Veterans Pension rates and Survivors Pension rates, checked November 9, 2026):

  • Veteran with no spouse or dependent: $29,093 a year, about $2,424 a month
  • Veteran with a spouse or dependent: $34,488 a year, about $2,874 a month
  • Surviving spouse with no dependent: $18,697 a year, about $1,558 a month

These rates change every December 1. VA ties its pension increase to Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment, and the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6% adjustment on October 14, 2026. If you're reading this after December 1, 2026, check VA's rates page for the new table rather than relying on the figures above.

For perspective, the national median cost of assisted living was $6,200 a month in the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey (released March 2026). Even the highest Aid and Attendance amount covers part of that, not all of it, so the rest would need to come from other income or assets, such as Social Security, a pension, savings or the sale of a home.

How to apply, step by step

  1. Gather documents. You'll want your parent's discharge papers (DD214 or equivalent), income records, bank and investment statements, and a list of out-of-pocket medical and care costs. For a surviving spouse, add the marriage certificate and the veteran's death certificate.
  2. Get the medical piece. VA Form 21-2680, the examination for housebound status or permanent need for regular aid and attendance, is filled out by your parent's doctor. VA's Aid and Attendance page explains the form and what other evidence helps.
  3. File the pension claim. If your parent doesn't already receive a VA pension, the Aid and Attendance request goes in with a pension application: generally VA Form 21P-527EZ for veterans or 21P-534EZ for surviving spouses. You can start online at VA.gov or work with a representative who files for you.
  4. Keep copies and track it. Save copies of everything you send and note the date. You can check claim status on VA.gov.

Filing can feel like a second job. That's exactly why free, trained help exists.

Get free help, and avoid paying for it

You should never have to pay to file an initial pension claim. VA's accredited representative FAQs (checked November 9, 2026) say representatives from Veterans Service Organizations never charge for help with a claim, and accredited attorneys and claims agents can charge only after VA has decided the initial claim, with a signed fee agreement on file.

VA also warns families about "pension poaching" in its pension poaching FAQs (April 2025), and the Federal Trade Commission (checked November 9, 2026) describes sellers who pitch annuities, trusts or paid "benefits planning" to help veterans qualify for pension, in ways that can tie up savings or cost a family its benefits. Be cautious if anyone:

  • Asks for an upfront fee or a percentage of benefits to file an original claim
  • Urges your parent to move or give away money quickly to qualify
  • Pairs "free" claim help with an annuity, trust or other product
  • Can't show up in VA's accreditation search

Good places to start:

  • Find a VA-accredited representative on VA.gov, and confirm anyone's accreditation there before you sign.
  • The Shelby County Veteran Services Office helps Memphis-area veterans and survivors with pension claims at no charge.
  • The Tennessee Department of Veterans Services works with county service officers statewide to help with claims and appeals.

Questions to bring to your benefits counselor

  • Does my parent's service meet the wartime and length-of-service rules?
  • Based on their income and assets, are they likely under the net worth limit?
  • Which of their care and medical costs could count toward lowering countable income?
  • Have any gifts or transfers in the past three years affected eligibility?
  • Should we apply now, or after a change in care or living situation?
  • How will the December 1 rate change affect the estimate?

Where private pay communities fit

As you compare options, ask each community how it handles payment. The Pointe at Kirby Gate, an assisted living and memory care community in East Memphis, is private pay: families pay the community directly, and the community does not accept or bill VA, Medicaid, TennCare or insurance companies. Questions about VA benefits belong with an accredited representative or benefits counselor, who can look at your parent's whole picture. Our current rates are published on our website at Rates and Floor Plans, and our guide to paying for senior living covers other options families consider.

Let's talk when you're ready
