Holidays and Dementia: Making Visits Meaningful

The holidays can feel different once a parent is living with dementia.

November 1, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 6 min read

The holidays can feel different once a parent is living with dementia. The meal is the same and the decorations come out of the same boxes, but the person at the center of so many family traditions may now find a full house loud, a change in routine unsettling, or a familiar face hard to place. Many families feel that gap most in November and December.

You are not alone in this. An estimated 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer’s in 2026, and nearly 13 million family members and friends provide unpaid care, according to the Alzheimer’s Association 2026 Facts and Figures report. November is also National Family Caregivers Month and National Alzheimer’s Disease Awareness Month, a good reminder that the people giving care need support too (Alzheimer’s Association).

The good news is that a meaningful holiday is still very possible. It usually just looks smaller, slower and simpler. Here is what the major dementia organizations suggest, and what families often find helps.

Start with expectations, yours and everyone else’s

The Alzheimer’s Association encourages caregivers to give themselves permission to do only what they can reasonably and safely manage. The National Institute on Aging makes the same point: accept that the holidays will be different, and plan around what is realistic.

That can mean letting go of the full menu, the long drive or the evening party. It can also mean deciding, as a family, which one or two traditions matter most and protecting those.

Talk to relatives before they arrive

Relatives who have not seen your parent in months may be surprised by the changes. The Alzheimer’s Association suggests a group call, video chat or email ahead of time so everyone understands what to expect and how to help. A few things worth sharing:

  • How your parent is doing now, in plain terms, and what tends to cause stress.
  • That they may not remember names, and that it is fine to gently introduce yourself (“Hi Mom, it’s your daughter”) instead of quizzing them.
  • That short visits often go better than long ones.
  • Who is the point person if your parent needs a break.

The NIA’s guide on helping family and friends understand Alzheimer’s has more ideas for preparing visitors, including bringing along a shared activity such as music or a photo album.

Plan the day around your parent’s best hours

Many people living with dementia do better earlier in the day. Confusion and agitation that grow in the late afternoon and evening, often called sundowning, are common, so the Alzheimer’s Association suggests celebrating at lunch rather than dinner when you can.

A few other adjustments that tend to help:

  • Keep the guest list small at any one time. The NIA recommends limiting how many visitors are present together. Staggered visits can let more people see your parent without a crowded room.
  • Set up a quiet room. A calm space with favorite music or a simple activity gives your parent somewhere to rest when the noise builds.
  • Protect the routine. Meals, medications and rest at the usual times matter more than the holiday schedule.
  • Avoid big changes in place. Unfamiliar homes, travel and crowds can be confusing, the NIA notes. If you do travel, the Alzheimer’s Association has travel tips for families.

Bring the traditions to them

Long-held traditions can still connect, even when recent memory fades. The NIA suggests building in the person’s own traditions and including religious or spiritual rituals they have known well. Ideas families use:

  • Play the music they loved for this season, and sing along if they like to.
  • Look through old holiday photos together and let them lead the story, even if details shift.
  • Give them a simple, real job: stirring a bowl, folding napkins, hanging a few ornaments, setting out cookies.
  • Bake or cook one dish from a family recipe and enjoy the smell together.
  • Read a familiar holiday passage, prayer or poem aloud.

The goal is a good moment, not a perfect day. If an activity frustrates your parent, it is fine to stop and try something else.

When your parent lives in a memory care community

If your parent lives in assisted living or memory care, the holidays can still be warm and personal. The Alzheimer’s Association suggests checking the community’s visitation policy ahead of time. A few more ideas:

  • Visit at their best time of day. Ask the care team when your parent is usually most rested and engaged.
  • Join in rather than take them out. A shared meal or activity in a familiar setting is often easier than a trip to a busy house.
  • Keep visits short and frequent. Two short visits can be better than one long one.
  • Decorate gently. A small, familiar decoration in their room can be comforting. Skip anything that looks like food, since the Alzheimer’s Association warns that candy-like decorations can be mistaken for something to eat.
  • Plan your goodbye. A calm, simple goodbye at a natural stopping point, such as after a song, often goes more smoothly than a long farewell.

At The Pointe at Kirby Gate, families are welcome to visit, share a meal and join activities. Our team is glad to talk through what tends to work best for your parent during a busy season.

Thoughtful gifts

The Alzheimer’s Association’s holiday gift guide offers ideas for both the person living with dementia and the caregiver. For your parent, simple, comforting and safe gifts usually work best:

  • A soft blanket, sweater or non-slip slippers.
  • A playlist or simple music player with songs from their younger years.
  • A photo album or framed photos with names written underneath.
  • An easy activity, such as a large-piece puzzle or a simple craft.

The association suggests avoiding dangerous tools or utensils, complicated electronics, challenging board games and pets as gifts.

For the caregiver in your family, the association says the most valuable gift is often time: an afternoon off, a meal delivered, help with errands or cleaning, or company at a support group.

Take care of yourself, too

Holidays add to a caregiver’s load. The NIA encourages caregivers to still enjoy the season and to ask someone else to sit with their loved one so they can step out. If you are feeling sad, it may help to know that many families feel a sense of loss during the holidays even while their parent is still here. Talking about it with a friend, a support group or a counselor can help.

If you need to talk to someone, the Alzheimer’s Association 24/7 Helpline is free and confidential at 800-272-3900, every day of the year.

A simple holiday checklist

  • Share an update with relatives before the holiday.
  • Choose one or two traditions to keep.
  • Plan the main gathering for lunch, not dinner.
  • Limit how many guests are in the room at once.
  • Set up a quiet room with familiar music.
  • Keep meals, medications and rest on the usual schedule.
  • Check visiting hours if your parent lives in a community.
  • Pick a safe, simple gift.
  • Ask for help and plan a break for yourself.
