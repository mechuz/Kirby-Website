Is Assisted Living or Memory Care Tax Deductible?

If you are helping pay for a parent's care, or managing the money they use to pay for it, a question tends to surface around this time of year: can any of this be deducted on a tax return?

November 13, 2026 · The Pointe at Kirby Gate · 9 min read

If you are helping pay for a parent's care, or managing the money they use to pay for it, a question tends to surface around this time of year: can any of this be deducted on a tax return? The honest answer is "sometimes, and often more than families expect, but only when specific conditions are met." This guide walks through how the federal rules work, in plain language, so you know what to ask a tax professional before the year ends.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice. Tax rules change and every family's situation is different, so please confirm your own situation with a CPA, enrolled agent or other qualified tax professional.

The short answer

Assisted living and memory care costs are not automatically deductible just because someone lives in a senior community. Under federal rules, some or all of the cost can count as a deductible medical expense when the care is for a person who is "chronically ill" and the services follow a plan of care prescribed by a licensed health care practitioner. Even then, the deduction only helps if the person filing itemizes deductions and their total medical expenses rise above a set share of their income, according to the IRS Topic No. 502, Medical and Dental Expenses (accessed November 2026).

Let's unpack each of those pieces.

What "qualified long-term care services" means

IRS Publication 502, Medical and Dental Expenses (2025 edition) lists amounts paid for "qualified long-term care services" as medical expenses. The definition comes from the federal tax code, 26 U.S.C. § 7702B(c), which describes them as the diagnostic, preventive, therapeutic, treating, mitigating and rehabilitative services, plus maintenance or personal care services, that are:

  • required by a chronically ill individual, and
  • provided under a plan of care prescribed by a licensed health care practitioner.

"Maintenance or personal care services" is the phrase that matters most for assisted living. It covers the everyday help many families are paying for: help with bathing, dressing, eating, moving from a bed to a chair, and similar daily tasks, when they are needed because of the person's condition.

Who counts as "chronically ill"

Under the same section of the tax code (26 U.S.C. § 7702B(c)(2)), a person is considered chronically ill if a licensed health care practitioner has certified, within the previous 12 months, that one of these is true:

  • They cannot perform at least two activities of daily living (eating, toileting, transferring, bathing, dressing and continence) without substantial assistance, for a period expected to last at least 90 days, or
  • They require substantial supervision to protect them from threats to health and safety because of severe cognitive impairment.

That second test is especially relevant for families considering memory care. A parent living with dementia may meet the cognitive impairment test even if they can still dress and feed themselves. Whether a particular person qualifies is a decision for their physician or other licensed practitioner, not for the community or for you, so start by asking their doctor whether they can provide the certification and a written plan of care.

Room and board: the "principal reason" rule

Families often assume only the "care" portion of a monthly bill could ever count, and that rent and meals never do. The IRS rule is more nuanced. IRS Publication 502 (2025) says that the cost of meals and lodging in a care setting can be included as a medical expense when the principal reason the person is there is to get medical care. If the main reason for living there is personal, such as convenience or wanting a smaller home, only the part of the cost that goes to medical or nursing care counts (IRS Publication 502, 2025; IRS Topic No. 502).

Publication 502 describes this rule in its section on nursing homes, so ask your tax professional how it applies to an assisted living or memory care bill. How much of a particular bill counts is a judgment call that depends on the facts: whether the chronically ill certification and plan of care are in place, why the person moved, and what records you can show. This is exactly where a tax professional's help matters.

The 7.5% threshold and itemizing

Even when expenses qualify, two hurdles decide whether they actually lower the tax bill:

  1. You have to itemize. Medical expenses are claimed on Schedule A (Form 1040), so the filer must itemize instead of taking the standard deduction.
  2. Only the amount above 7.5% of adjusted gross income counts. If a filer's adjusted gross income is $60,000, for example, the first $4,500 of medical expenses does not reduce taxes; only the amount above that does.

Both points come from IRS Topic No. 502 (accessed November 2026). Expenses that are reimbursed by insurance or another source do not count, and generally you can only include expenses actually paid during the tax year.

Because the cost of ongoing care adds up over a year, families who pay for it may find their medical expenses pass the 7.5% line. That is one reason this deduction is worth a conversation, even for people who have never itemized before.

If you pay for a parent's care

Many adult children pay part or all of a parent's bill. Whether you can deduct what you pay depends on your parent's relationship to your return.

When your parent is your dependent

You can include medical expenses you pay for a person who was your dependent either when the services were provided or when you paid for them (IRS Topic No. 502). The dependency rules for a parent, called the "qualifying relative" test, include a support test (you generally provide more than half of their total support) and a gross income limit that the IRS adjusts each year. Those rules are spelled out in IRS Publication 501, Dependents, Standard Deduction, and Filing Information.

Publication 502 has also long included a helpful exception: you may be able to include medical expenses you paid for a parent who would have been your dependent except that their gross income was too high, or they filed a joint return. Many parents receive pension or investment income above the gross income limit, so this exception can matter. Ask your tax professional to confirm how it applies in the current year's edition.

When siblings share the cost

If several brothers and sisters together provide more than half of a parent's support, but no one provides more than half alone, they can use a multiple support agreement to decide who claims the parent. According to IRS Publication 502 (2025), the person treated as providing the support can include the medical expenses they personally pay; the others in the agreement cannot include those same expenses. The publication's example is worth reading before your family decides who writes which checks.

Health savings accounts

If you or your parent have a health savings account (HSA), tax-free distributions can be used for qualified medical expenses of the account holder, a spouse and dependents, according to IRS Publication 969, Health Savings Accounts and Other Tax-Favored Health Plans (2025 edition). The same publication notes that you cannot also deduct on Schedule A any expense you paid with a tax-free HSA distribution. In other words, the same dollar cannot be used twice.

A newer deduction for older adults

Separate from medical expenses, the IRS describes a temporary additional deduction of $6,000 per person age 65 and older for tax years 2025 through 2028, which begins to phase out above $75,000 of modified adjusted gross income ($150,000 for joint filers). It is available whether or not the filer itemizes (IRS, "Check your eligibility for the new enhanced deduction for seniors", accessed November 2026). If your parent files their own return, this is worth mentioning to whoever prepares it.

A year-end checklist

With only a few weeks left in the year, here is a practical list to work through:

  • Ask your parent's physician whether they can certify chronic illness and provide a written plan of care, and keep a copy.
  • Gather every monthly statement and receipt for the year, and note who paid each one.
  • Ask the community for an itemized statement that separates care-related charges from other charges, if one is available.
  • Add up other medical costs too: doctor visits, prescriptions, dental, hearing aids and medical equipment can all count toward the same total.
  • If siblings share costs, talk now about who will claim your parent and whether a multiple support agreement makes sense.
  • Note any HSA distributions so expenses are not counted twice.
  • Book time with a CPA or enrolled agent before the deadline, and bring this list.

Common misunderstandings

  • "Assisted living is never deductible." Not true. When the chronically ill and plan-of-care conditions are met, some or all of the cost may count as a medical expense.
  • "Everything is deductible because Mom lives in a senior community." Also not true. The conditions above have to be met and documented.
  • "I can only deduct what I pay if Dad is my dependent." Not always. The gross income and joint return exception described above may still let you include what you paid.
  • "The whole bill comes off my taxes." A deduction reduces taxable income; it is not a dollar-for-dollar refund, and only the part above 7.5% of adjusted gross income counts.

Keeping the tax picture in perspective

A tax deduction can be a useful part of planning, but it should not be the reason a family chooses one setting over another. The right fit for your parent comes first: how much help they need day to day, how they feel about community life, and where they will be safe and engaged. Once you know what kind of support fits, the tax conversation becomes a planning step rather than a deciding factor.
