For many families, the house is the biggest thing a parent owns. It is also full of memories, which is why the question of selling it can sit unspoken for months. If your mother or father is moving to assisted living or memory care, the home is often the piece that makes the numbers work. It also raises practical questions that are hard to sort out at 11 p.m. on your phone: Will there be a big tax bill? Do we sell first or move first? What if the house takes months to sell?

This guide walks through the main issues in plain language, with links to the official sources, so you can have a clearer conversation with your parent, your siblings and a tax professional.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice. Tax rules depend on each person's situation, so please confirm the details with a tax professional or attorney before you act.

Start with the conversation, not the listing

Before anyone calls a real estate agent, it helps to agree on a few things as a family:

  • Is your parent part of the decision? If your parent is able to take part, their wishes come first. Selling a home someone has lived in for decades is a loss as well as a financial step, and it deserves time.
  • Who will handle the sale? If your parent cannot sign documents, someone needs legal authority to act for them. Check whether an existing power of attorney covers real estate, and ask an attorney or the title company early, not the week before closing.
  • What is the money for? Write down roughly how long the proceeds need to last and what else your parent has coming in each month, such as Social Security or a pension. That picture shapes every other choice.
  • Who else needs to know? Siblings who live out of town often feel shut out of decisions about the family home. A short call or shared notes can prevent hard feelings later.

Will your parent owe tax on the sale?

Often, less than families fear. The IRS allows many homeowners to exclude a large part of the gain on the sale of a main home from their income.

The basic home sale exclusion

According to IRS Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025 edition, checked November 16, 2026), a seller may be able to exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or up to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. To qualify, the seller generally must have owned the home and lived in it as a main home for at least 24 months out of the five years before the sale. The IRS summary in Topic No. 701, Sale of Your Home (checked November 16, 2026) adds that the exclusion generally is not available if the seller already excluded gain on another home within the two years before the sale.

"Gain" is not the sale price. It is roughly what your parent receives from the sale minus their adjusted basis, which starts with what they paid for the house (IRS Topic No. 409, checked November 16, 2026). Publication 523 explains how certain improvements, such as an addition or a new roof, can be added to basis. That is why old closing papers and receipts for major projects are worth hunting for in the file cabinet.

The rule that matters most when a parent moves to care

Here is the part many families have never heard of. Publication 523 includes a special rule for someone who becomes physically or mentally unable to care for themselves. If that person owned and lived in the home as their main home for at least 12 months during the five years before the sale, time spent living in a care facility licensed by a state to care for people with their condition can count toward the residence requirement.

In everyday terms: a parent who moves out of the house and into a licensed setting may still be able to use the exclusion even if the house sells more than three years later, as long as the conditions are met. The details matter, including whether the setting qualifies and how the dates line up, so this is a question to bring to a tax professional with your parent's move-in date in hand.

Paperwork to expect

Topic 701 notes that a seller who receives a Form 1099-S from the closing must report the sale on their tax return, even if the whole gain is excluded. Put a reminder on next year's tax checklist so the form does not get lost in the move.

Selling now, renting it out, or holding on

There is no single right answer. Each path has trade-offs worth talking through.

Selling

Selling turns home equity into money that can be set aside for care. It also ends the costs of an empty house: utilities, insurance, lawn care, repairs and the worry of a vacant property. The downside is emotional, and in a slow market the sale may take longer than you hope.

Renting the house

Renting keeps the home in the family and adds monthly income. Rental income is taxable, and ordinary rental expenses such as repairs can generally be deducted against it (IRS Topic No. 414, Rental Income and Expenses, checked November 16, 2026). Someone has to be the landlord, though, or pay a property manager. And because the home sale exclusion depends on how long the home was used as a main home within the five years before a sale, renting for several years can shrink or end the exclusion when the house is eventually sold.

Keeping the house in the family

Some families hold on to a home because of how inherited property is taxed. For most inherited property, the heir's basis is generally the fair market value on the date of death (IRS Instructions for Form 8949, checked November 16, 2026). That can be a real consideration, but it has to be weighed against paying for care today, carrying costs and the home sale exclusion your parent may already have. This is exactly the kind of trade-off a tax professional or estate planning attorney can run through with real numbers.

The timing gap: when care starts before the house sells

The hardest part for many families is timing. A parent's needs may not wait for the right buyer. Two common ways families bridge the gap are below. Neither is right for everyone, and both put the house up as security.

Bridge loans

A bridge loan is short-term financing secured by the current home and usually repaid when that home sells. NerdWallet (checked November 16, 2026) describes typical terms of roughly three to 12 months and notes that bridge loans generally cost more than a regular mortgage. A bridge loan can solve a timing problem. It does not solve an affordability problem, so it works best when the family is confident the house will sell and the proceeds will cover the loan.

Home equity lines of credit (HELOCs)

A HELOC lets a homeowner borrow against the home as needed. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guide, What You Should Know About Home Equity Lines of Credit (checked November 16, 2026), points out that HELOCs usually carry variable rates, that missing payments can put the home at risk of foreclosure, and that a borrower who sells the home is generally required to pay off the line in full. It is often easier to open a HELOC while the parent still lives in the home and has steady income, so this option takes planning ahead.

Whatever you consider, compare written terms from more than one lender, ask about fees and prepayment penalties, and make sure the person signing understands the commitment.

A practical checklist for the months ahead

  • Find the original closing statement and records of major improvements.
  • Note your parent's move-in date to any care setting, and keep the community's license information with your records.
  • Confirm who has authority to sign for your parent, and that the documents cover real estate.
  • Get a realistic market value from more than one local agent before setting expectations.
  • Call the homeowners insurance company and ask how the policy treats a home that sits empty.
  • Plan the downsizing: what moves with your parent, what goes to family, what is donated or sold.
  • Book time with a tax professional before the sale, not after, and an elder law attorney if there is any chance your parent will apply for public benefits later.
  • Keep the sale proceeds in a clearly labeled account so it is easy to see what is set aside for care.
