If you are looking at assisted living or memory care for a parent, you will hear the phrase "private pay" early and often. It simply means the family pays the monthly bill from its own resources. For most families that is not one big account. It is several smaller pieces that have to be fitted together: a Social Security check, perhaps a pension, some savings, and very often a house.

This guide walks through those pieces one at a time, shows a simple way to work out how long the money will last, and lists the questions worth asking before you sign anything. It is written for the son or daughter who has just been handed the paperwork.

This article is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Please talk with a tax professional, an elder law attorney or a financial planner about your own family's situation.

What "private pay" means

A private-pay community bills the resident or the family directly each month. It does not send the bill to a government program or an insurance company. Many families are surprised to learn that Medicare does not cover this kind of care. Medicare's own guidance says it does not pay for long-term care, which includes help with daily activities such as bathing, dressing and meals (Medicare.gov, long-term care coverage, checked November 2026).

The Pointe at Kirby Gate is a private-pay community. That makes planning more straightforward in one way: the question is not which program applies, but how your family's own income and assets line up against a known monthly cost.

Start with the real monthly number

Before you can plan, you need the full monthly figure, not just the starting rate. Nationally, the median cost of assisted living was about $6,200 a month in the most recent CareScout Cost of Care Survey (2025 data, released March 2026). Costs differ a good deal by city, by apartment size and by how much help a person needs, so treat a national figure as a starting point only.

Ask every community you visit for these details in writing:

  • The monthly rate for the specific apartment you are considering
  • What is included: meals, housekeeping, laundry, activities, utilities
  • Whether care is priced in levels, and what moves a resident from one level to the next
  • Any one-time fees at move-in
  • How often rates are reviewed, and how much notice families receive

Piece one: monthly income

Income is the foundation, because it arrives every month without anyone having to sell anything.

Social Security

The Social Security Administration estimates that the average retired worker will receive about $2,170 a month starting in January 2027, after a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (SSA cost-of-living information, announced October 2026). Your parent's amount may be higher or lower. Remember that the Medicare Part B premium is usually taken out before the check arrives. The standard premium for 2027 is $209.40 a month (CMS fact sheet, November 17, 2026).

Pensions and retirement accounts

Add any pension, and any regular withdrawals from retirement accounts. People who have reached the required age must take a minimum amount from traditional IRAs and most workplace plans each year, generally starting at age 73 (IRS, required minimum distributions, checked November 2026). Those withdrawals count as income for planning, and they are usually taxable.

Write down the total that actually lands in the bank each month. That is your income figure.

Piece two: savings and investments

Savings fill the space between income and the monthly bill. Gather statements for checking and savings accounts, certificates of deposit, brokerage accounts and retirement accounts, and add them up.

Two points are worth knowing before anyone starts withdrawing:

  • Different accounts are taxed differently. Money taken from a traditional IRA is generally taxed as income, while money in a regular savings account has already been taxed. The order in which accounts are used can change the tax bill.
  • Some care costs may be deductible. In certain situations, part or all of the cost of assisted living or memory care can count as a medical expense. The rules are in IRS Publication 502, and we explain them in plain language in our guide, Is Assisted Living or Memory Care Tax Deductible?

A tax professional can look at both points together. An hour of advice here often pays for itself.

Piece three: the house

For many Memphis families the home is the largest single asset. In July 2026 the median sales price in the Memphis area was $240,000, according to the Memphis Area Association of Realtors. There are three common paths.

Sell it

Selling turns the house into money that can pay for care for years. Federal tax law lets many homeowners exclude up to $250,000 of gain from the sale of a main home, or up to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if they owned and lived in it for at least two of the five years before the sale. There is also a helpful rule for people who move into a licensed care facility: time spent living there can count toward the residence requirement, as long as the person lived in the home for at least one year of the five (IRS Publication 523, checked November 2026).

Rent it

Some families keep the house and rent it out, adding the rent to monthly income. Rental income is generally taxable, and expenses such as repairs, insurance and property taxes are part of the picture (IRS Topic 414). Someone also has to be the landlord. Be honest about who in the family has the time.

Keep it for now

If a spouse still lives there, or the family is not ready to decide, the house can wait. Just include its running costs in your budget, because an empty house still has taxes, insurance and upkeep.

Our financing guide has more on each option in its section on using a home to pay for care.

Bridging the gap while the house sells

A move often has to happen before the house is sold. Families usually cover those months in one of three ways.

  • Savings first. Use cash on hand for the first months and refill it when the sale closes.
  • A home equity line of credit. This lets an owner borrow against the home and repay as they go, with the home as collateral. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau advises comparing it with other sources of money before deciding (CFPB home equity line of credit booklet, 2026 edition).
  • A short-term bridge loan. These loans are designed to be repaid when a home sells. They can be expensive and come with a firm deadline, so read the terms closely (Fortune, how bridge loans work).

Sometimes the simplest bridge is the family itself: a brother or sister covers two or three months and is repaid at closing. If you do this, put it in writing so that everyone remembers the same agreement.

How long will the money last? A simple worksheet

You do not need special software. Four steps on a sheet of paper will give you a useful estimate.

  1. Monthly cost. Write down the full monthly figure from the community.
  2. Monthly income. Write down what arrives in the bank each month.
  3. The gap. Subtract income from cost. This is what savings must cover each month.
  4. The runway. Divide total savings, including expected proceeds from the house, by the gap. The answer is the number of months the plan can run.

Here is an illustration using the national median, not any community's actual rate. Suppose the monthly cost is $6,200 and income is $2,900. The gap is $3,300 a month. If the family has $200,000 after selling the house and paying the costs of the sale, the plan runs for about 60 months, or five years.

Then test it. What if costs rise each year? What if more help is needed later? Run the numbers again with a higher monthly cost, and you will see how much cushion you have.

Have the family conversation early

Money decisions go more smoothly when everyone hears the same facts at the same time. A few suggestions:

  • Share the worksheet with brothers and sisters before a decision is needed.
  • Agree on who will handle the bills, and make sure that person has the legal authority to do so. An elder law attorney can explain what documents are needed in Tennessee.
  • Decide in advance what you will do if the plan needs to change.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What exactly is in the monthly rate, and what is billed separately?
  • How is a change in care level decided, and who is told?
  • When were rates last adjusted, and by how much?
  • How much notice is required if we need to move out?
  • Who do we call with a billing question?
