A study in JAMA Neurology looked at older adults who had no memory or thinking problems but whose tests showed Alzheimer's-related changes in the brain. Using long-running research groups, including the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, researchers estimated each person's chance of developing mild cognitive impairment or dementia over 5 and 10 years. Higher starting levels of a blood marker called p-tau217 were tied to a greater risk, and risk also rose with age. The goal is clearer, personal risk estimates instead of a simple yes-or-no result. Families with questions about memory testing should talk to their doctor.

Why it matters: Blood tests are entering memory checkups, so families may hear about these markers at a parent's next visit.
